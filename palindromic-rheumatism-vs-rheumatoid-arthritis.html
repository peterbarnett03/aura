Palindromic rheumatism vs rheumatoid arthritis

They share blood markers, they share joints, and one sometimes becomes the other. But day to day they behave very differently, and the difference is mostly about what happens between flares.

By Peter Barnett, who makes Aura · Updated 25 October 2026 · Not medical advice

The short answer

  • Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is persistent: inflammation sticks around and, untreated, can damage joints.
  • Palindromic rheumatism (PR) comes in attacks that resolve completely, with normal joints and normal X-rays in between.
  • Many people with PR test positive for the same antibodies as RA, and somewhere between a tenth and two-thirds go on to develop it, depending on the study.

Why they are linked

Rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP antibodies, the two blood markers most associated with RA, turn up in somewhere between 39% and 68% of people with palindromic rheumatism in most published series.1 Nearly one in five people with established RA report having had palindromic attacks before it settled in.1 That overlap is why researchers increasingly treat PR as an early point on the RA continuum, while noting that its imaging and clinical picture suggest the underlying process is not identical.2

How often does PR become RA?

Published figures range from 10% to 66%, which mostly reflects differences in who was studied, for how long, and on what treatment.1 Two examples:

  • In a Canadian series, 29 of 61 people (48%) had developed RA after an average of 5.4 years, and 83% of them had been anti-CCP positive at the start.3
  • In a Barcelona series, 22% had developed RA after an average of 7.6 years. Progression was more common in anti-CCP-positive people (30% against 15%), but most antibody-positive people still had not developed RA.4

Where it does happen, it tends to happen early: in one analysis the median time from the first palindromic attack to RA was about 1.2 years.1 Antimalarial treatment has been associated with slower and less frequent progression in an observational study, though not proven to prevent it.5

What to watch for

Without suggesting anyone diagnose themselves, these are the kinds of change worth raising with your rheumatologist rather than waiting for the next routine appointment:

  • attacks that last noticeably longer than they used to, or stop resolving completely;
  • joints that stay stiff or swollen between attacks;
  • more joints involved at once, or the same joints on both sides together;
  • attacks becoming much more frequent.
